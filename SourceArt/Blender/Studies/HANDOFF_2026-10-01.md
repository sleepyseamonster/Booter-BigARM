## Suggested next modeling pass

1. Capture a current baseline from the saved `.blend`: fixed close and surface views plus isolated solid/Workbench views of two or three representative formations from front, side, top, and a game-like elevated camera. Include a 5,000-triangle formation and one `Formation_00` rock with the dark material. Keep camera, lighting, and material settings identical for comparisons.
2. Inspect triangle placement and shading. Look for dense triangles on broad faces, high-frequency bumps that change the silhouette little, pinched normals, and small bevel-like rounding on every edge. Confirm these on the actual meshes before attributing all lumpiness to polygon count; material bump and smooth normals may also contribute.
3. Make a **temporary candidate**, not an immediate scene-wide conversion. Test larger, intentionally planar fracture faces with irregular but controlled edges. Spend geometry on silhouette breaks, ledges, contact edges, and meaningful cracks; use the existing material/normal detail for small-scale grain. Keep some weathering and chipped edges so the ancient stone is not a clean low-poly prism. Avoid radial center-fan fills, indiscriminate voxel remesh, blanket smoothing, and blind decimation that reintroduces tears or destroys strata.
4. Compare the candidate against the baseline from identical views. Record per-object triangles and the total, material readability, silhouette, visible flat-face area, and any defects. A lower number alone is not acceptance; the user wants a more believable stone surface at a sensible PC game source budget. Decide whether the method works before scaling it to the remaining formations.

If the candidate visibly improves the scene, apply it to the **currently open same file** through the Blender console, preserving transforms, materials, selection where practical, archived originals, terrain, and user-authored objects such as `Exposed shale plate 044.001`.
